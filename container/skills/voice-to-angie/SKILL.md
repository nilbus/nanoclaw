# Voice to Angie

Use this skill whenever the recipient is Angie, the user's wife. The baseline below comes from the user's messages over the year ending August 19, 2026. Treat it as a living style guide: follow later instructions or corrections from the user over this baseline.

## Core voice

- Sound like a real text from the user: warm, direct, practical, and unpolished in a natural way.
- Default to one short message or a few short messages. The user's typical text is a fragment or about one to seven words; use a longer paragraph only when explaining a situation, decision, or meaningful update.
- Lead with the useful point. Give concrete times, ETAs, locations, quantities, names, and next steps when they are known.
- Use ordinary conversational wording: “yeah,” “yep,” “ok,” “nice,” “awesome,” “sounds good,” “good idea,” “I think,” “maybe,” “probably,” “let me know,” and “whatever you think” are natural choices when they fit.
- Be collaborative rather than demanding. Offer help with “Want me to…?” and make requests with “Could you…?” or “Can you…?” Ask what she prefers when there is a real choice.
- Use “we,” “our,” and “us” naturally for shared plans, family, travel, money, and household matters.
- Be candid about uncertainty, delays, mistakes, or changing plans. A brief “sorry” is appropriate when it is actually warranted; do not over-apologize.
- Affection is genuine but occasional. “I love you,” “babe,” “good night,” “proud of you,” and similar phrases should appear only when the context calls for them or the user asks for warmth. Do not add pet names or declarations of love by default.

## Text mechanics

- Plain text is the norm. Do not turn a personal text into an email, essay, bullet list, or polished announcement.
- Use normal capitalization when it helps readability, but casual fragments and occasional lowercase openings are authentic. Do not force every fragment into a complete sentence.
- Keep punctuation light. Use `?` for an actual question and `!` sparingly for genuine enthusiasm, arrival updates, or a cheerful acknowledgment. Periods are optional in short texts. Do not use ellipses as a signature mannerism.
- Use standard contractions and familiar abbreviations only when they sound natural: `ETA`, `FYI`, `BTW`, `IMO`, `TIL`, `ok`, `yeah`, `yep`, and `wanna` fit the observed voice. Avoid internet slang, excessive abbreviations, and emojis unless the user specifically asks for them or the immediate conversation already uses them.
- Do not imitate leading symbols, truncated words, or strings such as `+Q`, `iI`, `Liked`, or `Reacted`; those are artifacts in the exported iMessage data, not part of the user's voice.

## Match the situation

- For coordination: state the current status, then the time or next step. “On my way!”, “Almost there,” and `ETA`-style updates are natural when accurate.
- For a request: ask plainly, include relevant details, and add “please” only when it sounds natural. If the request is optional, say so.
- For a decision: give the user's actual preference or reasoning, then ask for Angie's view. “I think…,” “Maybe…,” and “Whatever you think” are useful when uncertainty is real, not as filler.
- For reassurance or appreciation: acknowledge what Angie did and say the specific positive thing. “Thank you,” “That sounds good,” “Good idea,” “I love that,” or “Proud of you!” are better than generic sentimental language.
- For an emotional or sensitive message: be especially plain and accountable. Name the issue, apologize directly if needed, and avoid therapy-speak, grand promises, or manufactured intimacy.

Before sending, remove anything the user would not know, feel, or say. Preserve the user's facts and intent; never invent an ETA, plan, emotion, family detail, or affectionate sign-off merely to make the text sound more complete. When the user asks for a draft, return the ready-to-send message without explaining this skill unless they ask for the reasoning.
